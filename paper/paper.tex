\begin{abstract}
Weak or ambiguous requirements can give rise to several non-equivalent formal
specifications. A domain expert can narrow the candidates by classifying
concrete instances or traces on which they disagree. We study the symbolic
synthesis of complete distinguishing test batches: fixed suites that
collectively separate every candidate pair. All tests are synthesized in
advance of expert review, supporting asynchronous and parallel classification,
predictable workload, and reproducible decisions. The central challenges are
minimizing batch cardinality and scaling synthesis over an implicit symbolic
test space.

We formalize this problem and propose four synthesis methods, instantiated for
bounded Alloy models and temporal traces. Two cover-based methods synthesize
cardinality-minimum batches, either through joint MaxSAT optimization or by
enumerating the different ways feasible tests divide the candidates and then
solving an explicit minimum-cover problem over all candidate pairs. Two
tree-guided methods recursively partition the candidates using
feasibility-only SAT or balanced MaxSAT, reusing tests across branches.

Our evaluation covers 196 static and 32 temporal configurations. On paired
static configurations, reuse reduces balanced batch cardinality by 42\%
relative to the unreused construction tree, while exact minimization produces
batches a further 29\% smaller. The feasibility-only tree-guided method
completes every configuration in the main study, whereas the exact methods
exhibit different scalability
characteristics, particularly for temporal models. When used for adaptive
narrowing, balanced batches nearly attain the theoretical lower bound on mean
classification cost, while minimum-cardinality batches incur only a small
additional classification cost.

\keywords{Specification narrowing \and Symbolic test synthesis \and MaxSAT
  \and Alloy \and Temporal relational logic}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Developing a formal model may require choosing among alternative
specifications because weak or ambiguous requirements admit multiple
interpretations. Candidates may be written by different experts or produced
automatically. Program synthesis and repair, for example, may return several
artifacts consistent with incomplete input/output
examples~\cite{shriver2017end}; related techniques synthesize temporal
specifications from examples~\cite{gavran2020interactive,zhang2025constrained}
and repair relational
specifications~\cite{wang2019arepair,brida2021beafix,gutierrez2022icebar,cerqueira2022timely}.
More recently, LLMs have been used to generate formal specifications from
natural language, a process known as
\emph{autoformalization}~\cite{weng2025autoformalization,mendoza2026automating}.
Consistency with available examples does not establish which candidate
captures the intended requirement, and generating specifications from
potentially ambiguous natural language does not itself provide an independent
semantic oracle. Choosing among the candidates may therefore require
additional evidence from a domain expert, who may find concrete scenarios
easier to judge than the formal specifications themselves.

Specification narrowing obtains this evidence through concrete tests:
instances or execution traces on which candidates disagree. An expert
classifies a test as desirable or undesirable, eliminating candidates whose
predictions disagree with that judgment. In this paper, we study the synthesis
of a \emph{complete distinguishing test batch} for a given candidate set. A
batch is complete when every candidate pair disagrees on at least one of its
tests. Equivalently, each candidate has a unique pattern of acceptance and
rejection across the batch. Classifying the complete batch therefore leaves
at most one candidate consistent with the expert's judgments.

All tests in a batch are synthesized in advance of expert review. This
supports workflows in which tests are distributed to reviewers, classified
asynchronously or in parallel, or retained as a common basis for comparing
and revisiting judgments. The batch also makes the complete review workload
explicit upfront: its cardinality is the number of classifications required
for full review and an upper bound for adaptive narrowing. Existing online
narrowing approaches synthesize tests
in an interactive session, using each classification to guide the next synthesis
query. Complete batch synthesis must instead provide sufficient distinctions
for every possible intended candidate before any answers are available.

The central challenge is to synthesize a small complete batch at reasonable
computational cost. Each feasible test separates some candidate pairs, and a
complete batch must collectively cover all pairs. Given an explicit pool of
tests, minimizing batch cardinality is an instance of the classical NP-hard
Minimum Test Set problem~\cite{GareyJohnson,moret1985minimizing}. Here, however,
the test space is represented implicitly by a logical theory and analysis
bounds. A synthesis method must discover feasible tests as well as select
enough of them to distinguish the candidates. Even under finite bounds, this
space may contain exponentially many different ways of dividing the
candidate set.

We address this challenge through two families of symbolic synthesis
methods. The \emph{cover-based} methods directly target minimum batch
cardinality. \jointcover{} jointly synthesizes tests and selects a minimum
cover of the candidate pairs in one MaxSAT query. A second
cover-based method, \enumeratedcover{}, uses repeated SAT queries to
enumerate the different ways feasible tests divide the candidates, retaining
one representative test for each
division, and then solves the resulting explicit minimum-cover problem with
MaxSAT. Both methods return a cardinality-minimum batch whenever they
complete, but differ in the
symbolic models they solve and the amount of information they materialize.

The \emph{tree-guided} methods adapt the recursive candidate-partitioning
principle of existing adaptive narrowing
approaches~\cite{shriver2017end,gavran2020interactive,mendoza2026automating}
to complete batch synthesis. We develop two symbolic synthesis methods based
on this principle: \arbitrarysplit{} uses SAT to obtain any feasible split
and avoids optimization, whereas \balancedsplit{} uses MaxSAT to seek the
most balanced feasible split.
Both explore the two outcomes of every split before expert review and retain
generated tests for reuse across branches. Exploring both outcomes ensures
completeness; reuse allows a test synthesized for one candidate subset to
separate another subset without generating a new test. These methods provide
complete batches without guaranteeing minimum cardinality.

We formalize the batch-synthesis problem independently of a specification
language. We instantiate the four synthesis methods for Alloy's temporal relational
logic~\cite{jackson2012software,jackson2019alloy}. Our prototype uses
AlloyMax~\cite{zhang2021alloymax} for optimization and supports structural
instances and bounded temporal traces through an explicit lasso encoding. The
evaluation centers on two questions: how small the synthesized batches are,
including the contribution of reuse and the gap to the minimum; and how
synthesis time and completion depend on candidate count, structural scope,
and temporal bounds.

Our main benchmark comprises 196 static configurations derived from 28
requirements in three Alloy models, with candidate counts ranging from 4 to
28. A small temporal replication study adds 32 configurations from eight
requirements in one temporal model, with 4 to 16 candidates. On 167 static
configurations completed by both tree-guided methods and at least one exact
method, reuse reduces balanced batch cardinality by 42\% relative to the
unreused construction tree, while minimum batches are a further 29\% smaller.
\arbitrarysplit{} completes every static and temporal configuration in the main
study. The exact methods have different completion profiles: on the temporal
benchmark, \enumeratedcover{} completes 31 of 32 configurations, compared with nine
for \jointcover{}.

A complete batch can also support adaptive narrowing. After each
classification, an interactive tool can select an unused test from the
previously synthesized complete batch that most evenly divides the remaining
candidates and stop when at most one remains. Our secondary analysis shows
that balanced batches lie
within 0.004 classifications on average of the unrestricted binary-tree lower
bound on the completed static configurations. Even an optimal online synthesis
policy could therefore save at most this amount on average, and
minimum-cardinality batches incur only a small additional classification
cost. On these benchmarks, complete batches thus remain competitive in the
number of expert classifications. Preparing the complete batch requires
more upfront synthesis: on 176 paired static configurations, median synthesis
time is 1.65 seconds for the complete batch, compared with 0.82 seconds for
the mean online path. This additional effort moves synthesis before expert
review, potentially reducing interaction lag, and produces a complete, reusable suite.

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a formalization of complete distinguishing batches over an implicit
    symbolic test space, including their characterization as covers of
    candidate pairs;
  \item two exact synthesis methods that minimize batch cardinality through
    a single MaxSAT optimization or SAT enumeration followed by explicit
    minimum-cover solving with MaxSAT;
  \item two tree-guided synthesis methods, one using MaxSAT and the other using
    SAT, that adapt recursive candidate partitioning to complete batch
    construction with test reuse; and
  \item an evaluation of batch cardinality, synthesis cost, and scalability
    for structural instances and temporal traces, together with a secondary
    analysis of adaptive classification and the cost of complete
    precomputation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Distinguishing Test Batches}
\label{sec:problem}

We now formalize complete distinguishing batches and minimum-cardinality synthesis
independently of Alloy. Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} presents the four symbolic synthesis
methods, and Section~\ref{sec:implementation} instantiates them for Alloy.

\subsection{Candidates and Feasible Tests}

Let $F=\{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N\}$ be a finite set of candidate specifications
over a common vocabulary, and let $\mathcal I$ be the finite domain of tests
admitted by the background theory and analysis bounds. A test may be a static
instance or an execution trace; $I\models\phi$ means that test $I$ satisfies
candidate $\phi$. The domain $\mathcal I$ is represented implicitly by the
theory and bounds, rather than supplied as an explicit list of tests.
Two candidates are distinguishable within the bounds if some feasible test
satisfies exactly one of them. We assume throughout that the candidates are
pairwise distinguishable. Candidates that agree on all feasible tests cannot
be distinguished by test classification at the chosen bounds.

\subsection{Complete Batches and Minimum Covers}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{complete distinguishing batch} for $F$ is a finite set
$T\subseteq\mathcal I$ such that, for every two distinct candidates
$\phi_i,\phi_j\in F$, some $I\in T$ satisfies exactly one of them. A
distinguishing batch is \emph{cardinality-minimum} if no smaller such batch
exists.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, the candidates have distinct acceptance columns across $T$, as
illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:batch-matrix}. Completeness is a property of the
batch and candidate set, independent of which candidate is intended or which
judgments the expert will provide.

Let
\[
  U=\{\{\phi_i,\phi_j\}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq N\}
\]
be the candidate pairs, and associate each feasible test with the pairs it
distinguishes,
\[
  D(I)=\{\{\phi_i,\phi_j\}\in U\mid
          I\models\phi_i \Longleftrightarrow I\not\models\phi_j\}.
\]
A batch is complete exactly when its tests cover $U$. Minimum-cardinality
synthesis therefore solves
\[
  \min_{T\subseteq\mathcal I}|T|
  \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
  \bigcup_{I\in T}D(I)=U.
\]
Given an explicit pool of tests, this is the classical NP-hard
\emph{Minimum Test Set} problem~\cite{GareyJohnson,moret1985minimizing}. Here,
the test domain is implicit: synthesis must discover feasible tests as well
as select a minimum cover. Even under finite bounds, the domain may admit
exponentially many distinct ways of separating the candidates.

\subsection{Cardinality Bounds and Synthesis Objectives}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:batch-bounds}
For a pairwise distinguishable set of $N>1$ candidates, every minimum batch
$T^*$ satisfies
\[
  \lceil\log_2N\rceil\leq |T^*|\leq N-1.
\]
\end{proposition}

The lower bound follows because $k$ binary tests yield at most $2^k$
different acceptance columns. For the upper bound, start with all candidates
in one group and repeatedly choose a feasible test that splits a non-singleton
group, refining every group by its outcome. Pairwise distinguishability
ensures that such a test exists. Each step increases the number of nonempty
groups, so at most $N-1$ tests produce $N$ singleton groups and hence a
complete batch. Constraints on feasible tests may force the minimum above
the lower bound.

Our primary objectives are \emph{batch cardinality}, the number of distinct
stored tests $|T|$, and \emph{synthesis cost}, the total time required to
synthesize the complete batch. Full review requires exactly $|T|$ expert
classifications, so its classification count is already captured by
cardinality. Synthesis precedes expert review and is independent of the
intended candidate. Producing a smaller batch may require more synthesis
effort; we therefore compare methods by both batch cardinality and synthesis
time, including completion within a fixed budget as candidate counts and
analysis bounds increase.

\subsection{Reviewing a Synthesized Batch}

The tests in a complete batch can be classified independently and in any
order. A classification history is a sequence
$H=\langle I_1^{c_1},\ldots,I_k^{c_k}\rangle$, where $I_j\in T$ and
$c_j\in\{+,-\}$, with $+$ denoting desirable and $-$ undesirable. The
candidates consistent with that history are
\[
  C(H)=\{\phi\in F \mid
    I\models\phi \text{ for every } I^+\text{ in }H,\;
    I\not\models\phi \text{ for every } I^-\text{ in }H\}.
\]
After every test has been classified, completeness ensures $|C(H)|\leq 1$,
regardless of review order. A singleton identifies the candidate consistent
with all judgments; an empty set means that no candidate agrees with the
complete review.

\paragraph{Adaptive presentation.}
A precomputed batch can also be reviewed adaptively, stopping once at most one
candidate remains. For $C\subseteq F$, define
\[
  C_+(I)=\{\phi\in C\mid I\models\phi\},\qquad
  C_-(I)=\{\phi\in C\mid I\not\models\phi\}.
\]
A test \emph{splits} $C$ when both parts are nonempty. Let $C$ be the current
candidate set and $R\subseteq T$ the tests not yet classified. An adaptive
presentation policy $\rho$ maps every reachable state $(C,R)$ with $|C|>1$
to a test $\rho(C,R)\in R$ that splits $C$. For answer $c\in\{+,-\}$ to
$I=\rho(C,R)$, the next state is
\[
  \bigl(C_c(I),R\setminus\{I\}\bigr).
\]
A suitable test always exists: any two remaining candidates are distinguished
by a member of $T$, and previously classified tests cannot separate candidates
consistent with their answers. In Table~\ref{tab:batch-matrix}, for example,
$H=\langle T_1^+,T_2^-\rangle$ already gives $C(H)=\{S_3\}$, so adaptive
review can stop before $T_3$.

Our experiments use a greedy policy that minimizes
\[
  b_C(I)=\max\{|C_+(I)|,|C_-(I)|\},
\]
with deterministic tie-breaking. This policy aims for a short decision path
by ranking tests already in the batch. With a precomputed acceptance matrix,
selection and candidate updates require no further solving.

For the experimental analysis, we assume an intended candidate $\phi\in F$
serves as an \emph{oracle}, answering positively exactly when
$I\models\phi$. The \emph{adaptive classification cost}
$\mathcal C_\rho(F,T,\phi)$ is the number of requested tests before only the
oracle remains. Each splitting test eliminates at least one candidate, so
for $N>1$,
\[
  1\leq\mathcal C_\rho(F,T,\phi)\leq\min\{|T|,N-1\}.
\]
This cost is a secondary objective. A minimum cover need not include the test
that most evenly splits a particular remaining set, while balanced splitting
may retain tests unnecessary for distinguishing the candidates as a whole.
The evaluation examines whether minimum-cardinality batches incur a material
adaptive classification penalty.

\section{Symbolic Test Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The synthesis procedures require only a bounded propositional encoding of the
specification language. Let $X$ be the Boolean variables representing one test,
and let $B(X)$ characterize the feasible tests in $\mathcal I$, including the
background theory and analysis bounds. For each candidate $\phi_i$, let
$\widehat{\phi}_i(X)$ be true exactly when the test represented by $X$ satisfies
$\phi_i$. A satisfying assignment projected onto $X$ can therefore be decoded
into a concrete test. We write the encodings as arbitrary Boolean formulas;
conversion to CNF, including the introduction of auxiliary variables, is
standard.

Besides ordinary SAT, we use partial MaxSAT~\cite{cha1997local}, which maximizes
satisfied soft formulas subject to all hard formulas. This interface suffices
for every procedure below and is independent of the source language.
We specify each query by its set of hard constraints and, for MaxSAT, its
set of soft constraints.
The encodings below assume $N>1$; with at most one candidate, an empty batch
suffices.

\subsection{Cover-Based Batch Synthesis}
\label{sec:cover-based-synthesis}

The cover-based methods solve the minimum-cardinality problem from
Section~\ref{sec:problem}. Both require the selected tests collectively to
cover every candidate pair, differing in whether tests and their selection
are synthesized jointly or a finite pool of representatives is discovered
first.

\paragraph{\jointcover{}.}
Let $K=N-1$, the upper bound from Proposition~\ref{prop:batch-bounds}, and
create $K$ potential tests with disjoint variable copies $X^1,\ldots,X^K$.
Introduce validity variables $v_{k,i}$ for each test position and candidate,
and let $t_k$ indicate whether position $k$ is selected. The hard constraints
form the set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint-separation}
\begin{gathered}
  \{B(X^k)\mid 1\leq k\leq K\}
    \cup\{v_{k,i}\leftrightarrow\widehat{\phi}_i(X^k)
    \mid 1\leq k\leq K,\ 1\leq i\leq N\}\\
  \cup\\
  \Bigl\{\bigvee_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(t_k\land(v_{k,i}\oplus v_{k,j})\bigr)
    \;\Bigm|\;1\leq i<j\leq N\Bigr\}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The last family requires some selected test to separate each candidate pair.
The soft constraints are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint-objective}
  \{\neg t_k\mid 1\leq k\leq K\}.
\end{equation}
Maximizing their number minimizes the selected positions in one MaxSAT
query. Equation~\eqref{eq:joint-separation} ensures completeness. Conversely,
Proposition~\ref{prop:batch-bounds} guarantees that every minimum batch fits
within the $K$ positions, so an optimum yields a cardinality-minimum batch.
Before auxiliary variables for CNF conversion, the encoding uses $K$ copies
of $X$, $KN$ validity variables, $K$ selection variables, and $O(KN^2)$
separation occurrences. Replicating the bounded test encoding can therefore
be expensive when individual tests are complex.

\paragraph{\enumeratedcover{}.}
This method separates test discovery from cover selection. Tests with the
same acceptance vector separate the same candidate pairs; complementary
vectors also have identical pair coverage. One representative per distinct
candidate division therefore suffices for minimum-cover selection.

Define the Boolean vector $p(X)\in\{0,1\}^{N-1}$ relative to $\phi_1$ by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:separation-pattern}
  p(X)_i=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \bigl(\widehat{\phi}_{i+1}(X)
      \leftrightarrow\widehat{\phi}_1(X)\bigr),
  \qquad 1\leq i<N.
\end{equation}
For a concrete test $I$, write $p(I)$ for this vector evaluated on its
encoding. A bit is one when the corresponding candidate and $\phi_1$ agree
on the test. Thus, $p(I)$ records their relative agreement, independently of
which side accepts the test. The all-one vector $\mathbf 1$ represents
unanimous agreement and covers no pair.

After finding representatives $I_1,\ldots,I_m$, the next SAT query uses the
hard-constraint set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:enumeration-query}
  \{B(X),\ p(X)\neq\mathbf 1\}
    \cup\{p(X)\neq p(I_r)\mid 1\leq r\leq m\}.
\end{equation}
Each satisfying assignment gives a representative of a previously unseen
nontrivial division. Its blocking condition excludes every test with the
same pair coverage, including complementary acceptance vectors. When the
query becomes unsatisfiable, one representative has been retained for every
feasible nontrivial division.

Let $M$ be the number of retained representatives, and introduce a selection
variable $y_r$ for each one. The explicit cover encoding has the
hard-constraint set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:enumerated-cover}
  \Bigl\{\bigvee_{r:\{\phi_i,\phi_j\}\in D(I_r)}y_r
    \;\Bigm|\;\{\phi_i,\phi_j\}\in U\Bigr\},
\end{equation}
and the soft constraints
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:enumerated-objective}
  \{\neg y_r\mid 1\leq r\leq M\}.
\end{equation}
A MaxSAT optimum minimizes the selected representatives. Every test with
nonempty pair coverage can be replaced by its representative, and tests
covering no pair can be discarded.
These operations preserve completeness without increasing cardinality, so
this optimum is also a minimum over the implicit test domain.

The method uses $M+1$ SAT enumeration queries, including the final
unsatisfiable query, followed by one MaxSAT
cover query. Each enumeration query uses one copy of the bounded test
encoding, but $M$ can be as large as $2^{N-1}-1$. The number of queries and
the explicit cover can therefore grow exponentially with candidate count.

\subsection{Tree-Guided Batch Synthesis}
\label{sec:tree-guided-synthesis}

Both tree-guided methods start with a worklist containing $F$ and an empty
batch $T$. At each step, they remove a non-singleton candidate set $C$ and
select a feasible test that splits it, using a stored test or a new solver
query according to the method's reuse rule. A newly synthesized test selected
for the node is added to $T$, and both non-singleton parts are added to the
worklist. Stored tests can be reused only at nodes on other branches: a test
cannot split a descendant of a node it labels, since all candidates there
agree on its outcome. When the worklist is empty, $T$ contains the distinct
labels of the implicit binary construction tree.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:materialization}
For a complete tree-guided construction over $N>1$ pairwise distinguishable
candidates, the construction tree has exactly $N-1$ internal nodes and its
stored batch $T$ is distinguishing, with $|T|\leq N-1$.
\end{proposition}

Every internal node has two nonempty children and the leaves are the $N$
singleton candidate sets, giving $N-1$ internal nodes. Any two candidates
diverge at a node whose test distinguishes them, so the distinct node labels
cover every candidate pair. Reusing a label across nodes preserves this
coverage while reducing the number of stored tests.

\paragraph{\arbitrarysplit{}.}
At a node with candidate set $C$, this method reuses the first stored test
that splits $C$. If none exists, it asks a SAT solver to satisfy the
hard-constraint set
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:arbitrary-split}
  \Bigl\{B(X),\ \bigvee_{\phi_i\in C}\widehat{\phi}_i(X),\,
    \bigvee_{\phi_i\in C}\neg\widehat{\phi}_i(X)\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
The last two constraints ensure that the split yields two nonempty sets. The method requires no optimization and gives no
minimum-cardinality guarantee.

\paragraph{\balancedsplit{}.}
This method maximizes the number of candidate pairs separated at the current
node. For each $\phi_i\in C$, introduce a validity variable $v_i$. The
hard-constraint set is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:balanced-feasibility}
  \Bigl\{B(X),\ \bigvee_{\phi_i\in C}v_i,\,
    \bigvee_{\phi_i\in C}\neg v_i\Bigr\}
    \cup\{v_i\leftrightarrow\widehat{\phi}_i(X)\mid\phi_i\in C\}.
\end{equation}
The soft constraints are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:balanced-objective}
  \{v_i\oplus v_j\mid \phi_i,\phi_j\in C,\ i<j\}.
\end{equation}
Each soft constraint corresponds to an unordered candidate pair and is
satisfied exactly when the test separates that pair. Every candidate in
$C_+(I)$ is separated from every candidate in $C_-(I)$, giving
$|C_+(I)|\,|C_-(I)|$ satisfied soft constraints. Since the two parts sum to
$|C|$, maximizing this product is equivalent to minimizing the larger part,
$b_C(I)$.

For $m=|C|$, the encoding adds $m$ validity variables and $\binom m2$ soft
constraints to one copy of the bounded test encoding. The optimization
concerns the current split; it guarantees neither minimum batch cardinality
nor globally minimum adaptive classification cost.

Reuse preserves this local optimum. We first evaluate the stored tests on
$C$. If one attains the ideal value $b_C(I)=\lceil |C|/2\rceil$, we reuse the
earliest such test without a synthesis query. Otherwise, a MaxSAT query
establishes the best feasible split. If a stored test matches that optimum,
we reuse the earliest such test and discard the new witness; otherwise the
new test is stored. Every added test is evaluated against all of $F$, making
its acceptance values available for reuse across branches. Ideal reuse saves
both a synthesis query and a stored test; certifying a non-ideal reuse still
incurs the MaxSAT query while saving a stored test.

All four procedures depend only on $B$ and the candidate encodings
$\widehat{\phi}_i$. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes their bounded
Alloy instantiation; the solver-level account is independent of the SAT or
MaxSAT implementation.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Our evaluation addresses two main questions on complete batch synthesis and a
secondary comparison with adaptive iterative classification.

\textbf{RQ1 (Batch cardinality):} How small are the synthesized batches, how
much do reuse and minimum-cover optimization reduce their cardinality, and how
sensitive are these sizes to analysis scope?

\textbf{RQ2 (Synthesis cost and scalability):} What does synthesizing a complete
batch cost, and how do the methods scale with candidate count and analysis
bounds?

\textbf{RQ3 (Adaptive iterative classification):} How do complete batches
compare with iterative synthesis in classification effort and synthesis cost?

\subsection{Implementation and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:implementation}

\paragraph{Implementation.}
We implemented the four methods in a Python prototype using
AlloyMax~1.0.2~\cite{zhang2021alloymax}, with SAT4J for SAT and SAT4JMax for
partial MaxSAT. Given an Alloy domain model, candidate predicates, and analysis
bounds, the prototype returns tests as Alloy commands. Candidate acceptance is
checked by fixing each test's signatures and relations and solving the
corresponding predicate. Temporal formulas are encoded as bounded
lassos~\cite{biere1999symbolic}; the prototype currently excludes past operators
and mutable top-level signatures.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
The main benchmark contains 28 requirements from three static Alloy4Fun
exercises: a photo-sharing social network, a production line, and a train
station~\cite{macedo2021experiences,alloy4fun_dataset,a4f_semantic_dataset}. For
each requirement, we select the instructor's oracle and representatives of the
$N-1$ most frequent incorrect semantic classes, using each class's most
frequent syntactic submission and excluding unsupported representatives.
Candidate counts $N\in\{4,8,12,16,20,24,28\}$ yield 196 configurations. A small
temporal replication study uses eight requirements from Alloy4Fun's temporal
train-station model and $N\in\{4,8,12,16\}$, yielding 32 configurations. All
candidate sets are pairwise distinguishable at the default bounds. Sets
derived from the same requirement are nested, so our summaries are descriptive
rather than treating configurations as independent samples.

\paragraph{Protocol and measures.}

The default structural scope is~3, with maximum trace length~3 for temporal
models. Each complete batch has a 60-second synthesis budget. Experiments ran
on an Apple M5 machine with 24~GB of memory and macOS Tahoe. We measure
stored-test cardinality and wall-clock synthesis time; acceptance evaluation of
the completed batch and review simulation occur afterward and are excluded
from the budget. Cardinalities are averaged across configurations. A minimum
cardinality is certified by either cover-based method that completes. Runtime
comparisons report medians on configurations completed by every method shown;
each table identifies its comparison subset.

For RQ3, every candidate in turn acts as the oracle, accepting exactly the
tests it satisfies. Batch review follows the greedy adaptive policy from
Section~\ref{sec:problem} and stops when only the oracle remains.
Classification means weight oracle paths equally. The iterative baseline uses
the same balanced splitting objective but synthesizes a new test after each
answer. We evaluate all oracle paths by traversing its decision tree once,
charging each oracle only for synthesis and partitioning along its own path.
Iterative path times are averaged per configuration before computing paired
runtime medians.

Sensitivity studies vary structural scope from~3 to~7 for static sets with
$N=16$, using \arbitrarysplit{}, \balancedsplit{}, and \jointcover{}. Temporal
studies use $N=4$, varying either scope or maximum trace length from~3 to~7
while fixing the other bound at~3. Comparisons across bounds retain the same
configurations completed by every method shown at every bound. Full results
are available in the replication package.

The corresponding construction trees have 13.32 internal nodes on average.
Reuse reduces the stored batches to 8.54 tests for \arbitrarysplit{} and

Minimum covers contain 5.49 tests on average, a further 29\% reduction

candidates, fewer than nine tests on average suffice to distinguish every
candidate pair. Complete batch synthesis thus produces a compact suite for
full review even at the largest candidate count studied.

The small temporal replication shows the same reduction: on 29 paired
configurations, minimum batches average 4.21 tests compared with 5.90 for
\balancedsplit{}, again 29\% smaller.

\paragraph{Scope sensitivity.}
For $N=16$, the same 13 configurations complete with all three methods at
every scope from~3 to~7. Across these scopes, \balancedsplit{} averages
8.85--9.00 tests and \arbitrarysplit{} averages 9.38--10.54. The mean minimum,

candidate sets and completed subset fixed.
In the paired static scope study, scope~3 already achieves nearly the same
mean minimum batch cardinality as scopes~4--7. This finding is consistent
with Alloy's \emph{small scope hypothesis}~\cite{jackson2012software} and
supports the Analyzer's default scope of~3 as a practical starting point for
complete batch synthesis.

The temporal trace-length study also shows a stable minimum: on seven paired
configurations with $N=4$ at scope~3, it remains 2.14 tests as maximum trace
length increases from~3 to~7. Tree-guided means vary between 2.57 and 3.00
tests. RQ2 examines the synthesis cost of increasing these bounds.

\paragraph{Completion and candidate count.}
\arbitrarysplit{} completed all 228 static and temporal configurations at
the default bounds, each in at most 8.90~s. \balancedsplit{} completed all
static configurations through $N=16$, but only 17 of 28 at $N=28$. The
cover-based methods also become less robust as candidate count increases:
at $N=28$, \jointcover{} completed 14 configurations and
\enumeratedcover{} completed 11. Avoiding optimization therefore provides
the most reliable complete batches in this benchmark, at the cost of the
larger cardinalities reported in RQ1.

\paragraph{Cost of minimum covers.}
On the common static subset, all four median synthesis times are below
1.2~s, and \enumeratedcover{} has the lowest median, 0.66~s. Minimum batch
cardinality thus need not require longer synthesis when all methods succeed.
However, these medians do not capture the harder configurations that time
out. \enumeratedcover{} discovers a median of 33 distinct candidate
divisions (maximum 278) on its completed static runs; the reported time
includes both SAT enumeration and MaxSAT cover selection. Its exhaustive
discovery phase limits scalability as candidate count grows.

\paragraph{Temporal replication.}
\enumeratedcover{} completed 31 of 32 temporal configurations, compared
with nine for \jointcover{}. This difference is consistent with the cost
of replicating the bounded-lasso encoding up to $N-1$ times in
\jointcover{}, whereas each enumeration query encodes only one test.
The temporal sets also contain at most 16 candidates, so this small study
does not isolate the effect of temporal encoding from candidate count.
It suggests that enumeration is attractive when individual test encodings
are expensive and candidate sets are moderate.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to analysis bounds.}
On the 13 paired static configurations with $N=16$ from RQ1, increasing scope
from~3 to~7 raises median synthesis time from 1.44 to 4.84~s for
\arbitrarysplit{}, from 1.77 to 6.55~s for \balancedsplit{}, and from
2.26 to 23.31~s for \jointcover{}. Across all 28 configurations,
\jointcover{} completion falls from 24 to 13, while both tree-guided
methods still complete at least 26 at scope~7.

Increasing temporal trace length from~3 to~7 also raises synthesis time:
on the seven paired configurations with $N=4$, \arbitrarysplit{} rises
from 0.41 to 20.17~s and \enumeratedcover{} from 0.20 to 0.54~s.
Increasing temporal structural scope to~6--7 causes timeouts and heap
exhaustion even with four candidates.
The static scope and temporal trace-length studies therefore show that
larger bounds can substantially increase synthesis effort with little
reduction in complete batch cardinality.

\subsection{RQ3: Adaptive Iterative Classification}
\label{sec:rq3}

\paragraph{Classification effort.}
On the 176 static configurations completed by \balancedsplit{} (2,616
oracle paths), adaptive batch review and the iterative baseline both average
4.15 classifications, differing by less than 0.001. For $N$ equally weighted
candidates, $B(N)=k+2-2^{k+1}/N$, with $k=\lfloor\log_2N\rfloor$, is the
minimum mean depth of a binary decision tree, even if arbitrary binary
questions are available. The batch mean is only 0.0038 above this
path-weighted lower bound. On this subset, even an optimal iterative policy
could therefore save fewer than 0.004 classifications on average.

On the 132 static configurations completed by all four methods (1,632
oracle paths), the other three methods require only 0.031--0.035 more
classifications than the iterative baseline's mean of 3.890, despite their
different batch cardinalities. In the small temporal replication, the
\balancedsplit{} batch and iterative baseline have the same mean of 3.45
classifications across 288 oracle paths from 30 configurations. Complete
batches, including minimum covers, thus remain competitive with adaptive
iterative classification on these completed benchmarks.

\paragraph{Upfront synthesis cost.}
Median synthesis is 1.65~s for the complete \balancedsplit{} batch and
0.82~s for iterative synthesis on the 176 static configurations. Iterative
times average oracle-path costs within each configuration; the median
paired increase for complete synthesis is 0.65~s. On the 30 temporal
configurations, the corresponding medians are 3.86 and 2.47~s.
These comparisons exclude the batch timeouts reported in RQ2.
The extra effort delivers a complete reusable suite before review, with
adaptive presentation requiring no further synthesis.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
The educational models, nested candidate sets, and selection of frequent,
supported semantic classes from student submissions limit generalization to
industrial or GenAI-generated candidate pools. The temporal replication
covers only one model. Minimum cardinalities and observed scope stability
are relative to the analyzed bounds and fixed candidate sets. Cardinality
measures the number of tests; expert comprehension and review time remain
unmeasured. RQ3 uses one balanced iterative baseline under the same encoding,
weights candidates equally as oracles, and assumes that the intended
candidate is present and answers are error-free. Synthesis times depend on
the encoding, solver versions, and hardware, and exclude acceptance evaluation
of the completed batch. Paired cardinality and runtime summaries condition
on completion and can favor easier configurations. We use one toolchain,
retain common comparison subsets, and report completion separately.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Minimum test sets and suite synthesis.}
Given an explicit finite test pool, Minimum Test Set selects a smallest subset
that distinguishes objects by their test
outcomes~\cite{GareyJohnson,moret1985minimizing}. Our candidate-pair cover
objective has this form, with candidates as objects and acceptance as test
outcomes. The symbolic setting adds the problem of discovering feasible tests.
\jointcover{} represents test generation and cover selection in the same
MaxSAT problem, while \enumeratedcover{} first materializes the different
feasible candidate divisions and then selects a minimum cover.

Related suite optimization targets program coverage. Test-suite reduction
retains existing tests that preserve a chosen coverage
criterion~\cite{khan2018systematic,hsu2009mints}. Whole-suite generation
optimizes coverage and suite size~\cite{fraser2012whole}. SAT-based
approaches generate minimum suites for modified condition\slash decision
coverage~\cite{kitamura2018optimal,yang2018generating}. Our coverage elements
are candidate pairs, and feasible tests are defined by the background theory
and analysis bounds.

\paragraph{Interactive narrowing.}
Shriver et al.~\cite{shriver2017end} narrow program candidates using generated
inputs whose expected outputs are supplied by the user.
LTLTalk~\cite{gavran2020interactive} similarly generates distinguishing
behaviors to refine temporal specifications. ARTEMIS, by Mendoza et
al.~\cite{mendoza2026automating}, seeks balanced distinguishing traces to
reduce the number of user judgments. PICK~\cite{prasad2026pick} presents
distinguishing scenarios for GenAI-generated regular expressions,
access-control policies, and temporal formulas, incorporating uncertain
answers and elimination thresholds and evaluating the workflow through user
studies.

These approaches use feedback to guide subsequent question generation. Our
tree-guided methods adapt the recursive partitioning principle to complete
batch synthesis: they explore both outcomes before review and reuse stored
tests across branches. The resulting objective concerns the number of distinct
stored tests and the synthesis effort needed to prepare the entire suite.

\paragraph{Decision trees and state identification.}
The expected number of questions is a standard objective for decision trees;
minimizing it is NP-hard~\cite{hyafil1976constructing}. Generalized Binary Search greedily
chooses a query that most evenly splits the remaining
hypotheses~\cite{nowak2011geometry}. This criterion underlies the local
objective of \balancedsplit{}, but local balancing does not minimize the
number of distinct stored tests.

FSM state identification uses observed outputs to identify an unknown state
through preset or adaptive distinguishing sequences. Hierons and
T\"urker~\cite{hierons2015incomplete} study small collections of incomplete
sequences that collectively distinguish all states. This relates to collective
coverage by a batch; in our setting, the outcomes are expert judgments on
instances or traces of competing specifications.

\paragraph{Tests from formal specifications.}
AUnit~\cite{sullivan2017automated} generates Alloy tests for coverage and
mutation analysis. TestEra~\cite{marinov2001testera} uses Alloy specifications
to generate tests for Java programs, while model-based
testing~\cite{dalal1999model} derives tests from system models. Our adequacy
criterion requires a collection of tests that distinguishes every pair in a
given candidate set.

\paragraph{LLM-generated validation tests.}
Cunha and Macedo~\cite{cunha2025validatingformalspecificationsllmgenerated}
evaluate the generation of positive and negative Alloy tests from natural
language requirements. Such tests can filter candidate specifications by
checking their agreement with the expected outcomes, but several distinct
candidates may still pass the same suite. The techniques are complementary:
after initial filtering with LLM-generated tests, our methods could synthesize
a complete distinguishing batch for the surviving candidates. Expert review
of this additional batch would then resolve the distinctions left open by the
initial suite.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We formalized complete distinguishing batches as covers of candidate pairs
and developed four symbolic synthesis methods, instantiated for Alloy
instances and bounded temporal traces. The cover-based methods minimize
cardinality, while the tree-guided methods reduce stored tests through reuse
across branches. Complete batches provide a fixed suite before expert review,
make the full review workload explicit upfront, and support independent or
parallel classification.

On 167 paired static configurations, reuse reduces \balancedsplit{} batch
cardinality by 42\%, and minimum covers are a further 29\% smaller. With 28
candidates, minimum covers average 8.69 tests on the 13 paired configurations.
In the paired static scope study, scope~3 already yields nearly the same
minimum cardinality as larger scopes, which require substantially more
synthesis effort. \arbitrarysplit{} completes all 196 static and 32 temporal
configurations at the default bounds, while the cover-based methods have
different completion profiles, particularly for temporal models.

Adaptive presentation of pre-computed batches remains competitive with iterative narrowing: balanced
batches nearly attain the theoretical lower bound on mean classification
cost, and minimum covers incur only a small additional cost. The extra
upfront synthesis effort produces a complete reusable suite whose adaptive
review requires no further synthesis.

Future work will extend the evaluation to realistic case studies, particularly
those with larger sets of temporal requirements. User studies will assess
expert review effort and guide the tuning of optimization criteria to account
for the difficulty of understanding and classifying individual tests.
